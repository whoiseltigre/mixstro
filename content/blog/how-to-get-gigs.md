**The short answer:** to get gigs, get a tight 30 to 45 minute set ready, record it, and put your music, photos and dates in one place a booker can open in seconds. Then start where the door is already open: open mics, jam nights and shows with local bands. Email venues that book your size and genre, bring people, and get asked back.

That's the loop most working musicians run, whether they play originals in a three-piece or covers at weddings. I've played guitar for more than 20 years, in a lot of different lineups and rooms, and the first gig is always the hardest one to land. After that, gigs mostly come from other gigs. If you're still putting the band together, start with our guide on [how to start a band](/blog/how-to-start-a-band), then come back here when you've got songs to play.

## What do you need before you ask for gigs?

A booker is deciding two things: will this act be good, and will anyone come? Have answers to both before you send a single message.

![A singer and guitarist playing a small bar show with the audience close to the stage](/site/v1/blog/how-to-get-gigs/photo-1-fe3a486f "Photo: RDNE Stock project|https://www.pexels.com/photo/audience-looking-at-a-man-and-a-woman-on-stage-6173859/")

- A set you can play without stopping. For most first shows, that's 30 to 45 minutes. Rehearse the transitions and the tuning breaks too, not just the songs.
- Recordings. A clean live video of one or two songs often does more than a polished studio track, because it shows the booker exactly what they'll get on the night. Phone audio is fine if the playing is good.
- A few good photos. Something a venue can put on a poster or an Instagram post without asking you for something else.
- One link with everything. An [electronic press kit](/blog/electronic-press-kit) is the standard: a short bio, music, photos, past shows and contact details. It doesn't need to be fancy. It needs to open fast on a phone.
- A rough idea of your draw. Be honest with yourself about how many people would come to see you on a Thursday. Ten real people is a fine start. Bookers care more about honest numbers than big ones.

If you're a solo player looking for hired work instead of booking your own shows, the same idea applies. Bandleaders want to see what you've played and who with, which is what a [musician resume](/blog/musician-resume) is for.

## Where can musicians find gigs?

Look in several places at once. Most of these cost nothing but your time.

### Open mics and jam nights

This is where nearly everyone starts. You don't need to book anything. You sign up, play two or three songs and learn how a room reacts. More importantly, the person running the night often books the venue's other shows too, and the musicians in the room are the ones who'll offer you your first support slot. Go regularly, stay for other people's sets and talk to them.

![A singer playing acoustic guitar at an open mic under stage lights](/site/v1/blog/how-to-get-gigs/photo-2-7aed9fc9 "Photo: Viktorya Sergeeva|https://www.pexels.com/photo/woman-standing-and-playing-guitar-and-singing-15789086/")

### Other local bands

Shows are usually three or four acts on one bill, and bands put those bills together themselves all the time. Go to local shows, buy merch, follow the bands you like and tell them you'd love to share a bill. When you get a show of your own, offer them a slot. Trading shows with a band from a nearby city is one of the oldest ways to start playing outside your hometown.

### Bars, cafes and small venues

Find the rooms that already book acts your size. Look at their event listings or social media for the last couple of months. If every band on there has a big following, it isn't your first stop yet. If you see local names and weeknight shows, it is. Breweries, restaurants, cafes, bookshops and record stores all book music too, and they're often easier to get into than clubs.

### Promoters and talent buyers

In bigger cities, many venues hand their calendar to local promoters. Find out who books the shows you go to (the poster or event page usually says) and get on their radar. Promoters remember acts that bring people and are easy to work with.

### Private events, weddings and corporate work

If you play covers, this is where the steadier money is: weddings, birthdays, company parties and holiday events. Event planners and wedding venues keep lists of acts they trust, so introduce yourself to them directly. A short video of your set and a clear song list help a lot here.

### Musician networks and apps

A lot of gigs never get posted publicly. A guitarist gets sick, a band needs a fill-in drummer for Saturday, a singer needs a pianist for a wedding. These go to whoever the person already knows. The more musicians know what you play and that you're available, the more of these calls you'll get.

## How do you book a gig at a venue?

Find the right person, send a short email and follow up once. That's it. Most booking happens over email, not in person at the bar on a busy night.

1. Find the booker. Check the venue's website for a booking email or form. If there isn't one, call during the day and ask who books the music.
2. Pick dates. Ask for specific weeks, not "any time". Local venues often book six to ten weeks ahead, sometimes more.
3. Send a short email. Use the template below. Keep it to what fits on a phone screen without scrolling much.
4. Follow up once, about a week later. A polite one-line nudge is normal. If there's still no reply, try again in a month with different dates.

Some venues only book through a form or a booking platform. Use whatever they ask for. Skipping their process won't get you in faster.

### A booking email you can copy

> **Subject:** Booking inquiry: [Band name], [genre], [city], [month]
>
> Hi [name],
>
> We're [band name], a [genre] [duo/three-piece/band] from [city]. We'd love to play [venue] in [month], ideally a [weeknight/weekend] around [dates].
>
> We sound like [two artists people know]. Here's a live video from our last show: [link]. Everything else, including photos and past shows, is here: [EPK link].
>
> Last month we drew about [number] people at [venue or event]. We can bring a full bill with [other band], or happily open for one of your regulars.
>
> Thanks,
> [Name], [phone]

Change the details for every venue. A booker can tell a mass email in about one line, and the one sentence that shows you know their room does more than everything else in the message.

## How do you get paid gigs?

Your first few shows might pay nothing or very little, and that's normal for originals bands. Paid gigs come from two things: a draw you can prove and a set that fits what the venue needs. Know the common deals so you can tell a fair offer from a bad one.

- Door deal. The band gets all or part of the money taken at the door, sometimes after the venue's costs. Common for local originals shows.
- Guarantee. A flat fee, whatever the turnout. Normal for covers, private events and bands with a proven draw.
- Guarantee versus door. You get the flat fee or your share of the door, whichever is higher.
- Ticket split. On ticketed shows, the bands split ticket sales after costs, often weighted toward the headliner.
- Pay-to-play. You pay the venue or promoter, or buy tickets up front to resell. Be very careful with these. They usually mean the promoter's risk has been moved onto you.

Ask what the deal is before you agree to a show, and get it in writing, even if it's just an email. Ask what time load-in is, how long your set is, whether there's a sound engineer and whether you get any free drinks or a guest list. None of this is rude. It's how professionals book.

If you play your own songs, sign up with a performing rights organization (like ASCAP or BMI in the US, or PRS in the UK) and report your setlists. Live performances of your originals can earn royalties on top of whatever the venue pays.

## How do you get booked again?

Most of your future gigs will come from the people at your last one, so treat every show as an audition for the next.

![A rock band playing on a club stage under purple and blue lights](/site/v1/blog/how-to-get-gigs/photo-3-539260ef "Photo: Matthew Kalapuch|https://unsplash.com/@matthewkalapuch")

- Promote the show. Post about it more than once, make a simple event page and personally invite people. Don't leave it all to the venue.
- Be easy to work with. Show up on time, load in fast, play your agreed set length and get off stage on time. Sound engineers and bookers talk.
- Bring people and keep them. Have a QR code or a sign-up sheet for an email list, and sell something at the merch table, even if it's just stickers.
- Thank everyone. The booker, the sound engineer and the bands you played with. Ask the booker directly before you leave if they'd have you back.
- Write it down. Keep a list of where you've played, the crowd you drew and who booked you. It's the proof you'll use in your next booking email.

## How do you get gigs with no experience?

Start smaller than you think, and say yes to almost everything early on. Open mics, jam nights, house parties, school and community events, charity shows and opening slots for friends' bands all count. Each one gives you a better video, a better story for your next email and a few more people who'll come to the next show.

Busking is worth a try too, if your city allows it. Some cities need a permit, so check first. It's free practice in front of strangers and you'll learn quickly which songs stop people walking.

You'll also get more gigs by playing in more than one project. Plenty of musicians play in an originals band, a covers band and fill in for other bands. If you're open to that, our guide on [how to find band members](/blog/how-to-find-band-members) works just as well in reverse: the same places bands look for players are where you get found.

## What are the most common gig booking mistakes?

- Asking for a Saturday headline slot first. Start on weeknights and opening slots, and earn the weekend.
- Sending the same email to fifty venues. Bookers can tell, and they rarely reply to them.
- Making the booker work. No link, a link that needs a login, or a 20 MB attachment usually means no reply.
- Inflating your draw. If you say 80 and bring 12, you won't be back.
- Booking shows too close together in the same town. Your friends won't come to three shows in one month. Space them out.
- Leaving the money vague. Agree on pay and set times before the show, not at the end of the night.

## Keep the next gig coming

The bands that play the most aren't always the best ones. They're the ones other musicians and bookers remember and can find easily. Once you've played a few shows, put them where people can see them, keep in touch with the bands you shared a stage with and keep your music easy to find.

## FAQ

### How do beginner musicians get gigs?

Start with open mics and jam nights, then ask local bands for opening slots and play small rooms like cafes, breweries and bars that book weeknight music. Record a live video at every show so your next booking email has proof of what you sound like.

### How do I get my first gig as a band?

Rehearse a 30 to 45 minute set, record a live video and put together a simple press kit. Then email venues that book bands your size, ask local bands to share a bill, or put on your own show with two other bands and split the work of promoting it.

### How far in advance should you book a gig?

For local bars and clubs, six to ten weeks ahead is common. Bigger venues and festivals book months in advance, and private events like weddings are often booked a year or more out. Last-minute openings come up too, which is why it pays to know other musicians.

### How much should a band charge for a gig?

It depends on the city, the kind of gig and your draw. Local originals shows often pay a share of the door, while covers gigs, weddings and corporate events usually pay a flat fee that's much higher. Ask other local musicians what they get paid for similar gigs before you name a price.

### Should you ever play for free?

Sometimes. Open mics, charity shows and a support slot that puts you in front of the right audience can be worth it early on. Be wary of venues that make money from your show and pay you nothing, and avoid deals where you pay to play.

### What do venues look for when booking bands?

Venues want acts that fit their room and their audience, will bring people through the door and are easy to work with. A good live video, honest numbers about your draw and a short, specific email do most of the work.
